House tank › Plan

The tank feeds the house

City water is too weak to run the showers, so you store it in the tank and let a pump push it to the faucets.

Here is the part that is easy to get wrong. When the pump is off, water does not keep flowing through it. The pump is a door that slams shut. That is why you add a second pipe that goes around the pump.

The pump is a closed door

Picture a door with a spring on it. While the pump is running, it pushes that door open and water goes to the house. The moment the pump stops, the spring slams the door.

City water is then standing on the wrong side of a shut door. It cannot reach the faucets. A little drip sneaking past does not count. The shower will not work.

At the store this door is called a check valve. You can forget that name until you are standing at the counter.

So you give the house a second way in

One way is through the pump. That way only works while the pump is on and pushing.

The other way is a plain pipe that never enters the pump. Call it the backup pipe. The store calls it a bypass. On a normal day the tap on that pipe stays closed, because you want the pump doing the work.

The backup pipe is not there to help the pump. It is there for the hours the pump is off, broken, or you ran out of daylight.

The only two taps you have to remember

The tank tap sits on the pipe that climbs up to the tank. Open, the tank fills. Closed, it does not. That is its whole job. The store calls it a fill valve.

The backup tap sits on the pipe that skips the pump. Closed on a normal day. You open it only after the pump is unplugged.

When you need that slow water, do these three things in order. The order matters. Water leaves the tank through the bottom. The pump pushes it. An open backup tap gives that push a road back to the meter. The inlet on top ends in open air, so the tank cannot send water back out of it.

  1. Unplug the pump.
  2. Close the tap on the pipe that leaves the pump.
  3. Open the backup tap.

You can skip the backup pipe

Skip it if you are fine with every faucet going dead whenever the pump is off or the power cuts out.

You can also skip it on Saturday if you can put the old pipe back before dark, and add the backup pipe another day. Once the house only gets water through the pump, a dead pump means no water until you cut the pipe again.

Can city water reach the tank?

Height is from the meter up to where water spills into the tank. Pressure is the gauge with the other taps closed. Feet of height ÷ 2.31 = psi to reach. You want about 5 psi spare.

Enter both numbers. Feet of height ÷ 2.31 = psi to reach the tank. For meters, turn them into feet first (× 3.28). You want about 5 psi, or 0.3 bar, more than that, so a bend and a neighbor's tap don't stall the fill.

A few things that will ruin Saturday if you skip them

The tap under the tank has to shut

That tap is already there. Open it into a bucket, standing to the side, then close it. A full tank pushes hard. If it will not stop, Saturday is about that tap, and the house stays on city water.

Find out if city water can reach the tank

What gets water up the tower is pressure, the same way a weak speaker cannot fill a big room no matter how wide the cable is. A fatter pipe does not make weak pressure climb higher. It only fills faster once the water can reach.

Screw a gauge on a hose tap, close the other taps, and type the number above. A slow fill is a win. The tank is the storage. The pump is what makes the shower strong. Buy a second pump only if the number says the water cannot reach.

The tank has to know when it is full

On a quiet night the city pressure rises and the tank will keep filling until it spills. A float is a ball that rises with the water and shuts the pipe. An overflow pipe is the backup if that ball sticks. Point the overflow where you can see it, away from the tower legs and away from the plug.

Let the fill pipe pour in from above, with a finger-width of air between the pipe and the water. If that pipe is sealed under the water, a drop in city pressure can suck your tank back into the neighborhood line. Leave the lid on, but do not seal the tank like a drum. It has to breathe.

Don't choke the pump

The pipe from the tank down to the pump should stay as fat as the hole on the pump, the whole way. Necking it down to a skinny pipe is like asking someone to breathe through a straw. The pump gasps, and that sound is it tearing itself up. If the house pipe is smaller, make it smaller once, on the house side of the pump.

Old tanks hold grit at the bottom. A strainer catches it. Hang the little cap downward so you can unscrew it over a bucket.

The backup pipe, one more time

The pictures are above. Unplug the pump, close the tap on the pipe leaving the pump, then open the backup tap.

The plug has to be able to trip

Press Test on the outlet. It should go dead. Press Reset. Power should come back. If it stays dead, that outlet is not the one you use. Outside, the cover has to close over the cord, and the cord should dip so rain drips off the loop instead of into the plug. The number on the pump box has to match the outlet. If powering it means opening a wall, that part waits. Saturday can still be the pipes.

Take the joint apart before you cut it

A lot of these come apart with two wrenches. Try that first. On plastic, a ratchet cutter leaves a clean end a fitting can grab. The meter belongs to the water company. Close their valve, and work on your side of it.

Build the new parts while the house still has water. White tape is only for screw threads. If you glue a joint, the can tells you how long to wait, so glue those early.

Close the meter only when these are true

Checks save on this phone. The house still has water until you choose to close that valve.

Leave the meter open. The house still has water.

What to buy

Write the sizes down before you pay. Check off anything already in the truck.

Sizes first

Your sketch already has the pump, the pipe, and maybe a one-way valve. Keep those. The filter "Still to get" is what you carry to the counter.

Buy the pump before the last fittings, and hold every thread up to the real ports. Threads that start by hand are the right threads. A hard bind means they don't match. Forcing one cracks the pump.

Write the sizes down before you pay. A valve in the wrong size is a second trip.

Read the printing on the pipe. If it says 1/2, 3/4, 1, or 20, 25, 32, ask for that. Smooth ends are for cement. Threads get tape. Steel looks gray. Copper looks brown. If the pipe is the fused green or white kind, or black poly, cement will not hold it. Ask for the adapter made for that pipe.

On the pump shelf, before you pay

Tell the clerk: the tank is above the pump, the house pipes are small, and you want a house pump that stops when the taps close and stops if the tank is empty. A huge pump will find every weak joint in the walls.

If the box already includes a one-way valve, skip an extra one on the pump. If it says nothing, buy one, arrow toward the house, and put any shutoff after the controller. The city-side one-way valve is separate. Buy that either way.

Say this at the counter

Ask for a tap for the pipe that fills the tank, and a tap for a backup pipe that goes around the pump. The store calls those a fill valve and a bypass. The pictures of what they do are on Plan.

House tank › Day

The day

One person owns the meter. One person owns the tower.

Who does what

Swap those jobs only in the morning, if one of you is happier on the ladder. After that, the meter person stays at the valve during the shutdown. Tell anyone else in the house to leave the taps alone until you say so. Tape a note on the meter: working, leave it.

If the day goes bad, the backup tap is how the house gets water. Unplug the pump, close the tap on the pipe leaving the pump, then open the backup tap. The pictures are on Plan.

The hours

First hour

Decide before anyone cuts

On the ground

Gauge on a hose bibb, other taps closed, number into the calculator. Photos of the meter outlet, the house pipe, and the outlet. Press Test, then Reset.

On the tower

Measure up to the inlet. Photos of the inlet, the bottom valve, and the lid. Crack the valve into a bucket from the side, then close it and watch it stop.

Stop the whole cutover if the valve won't seal or the water can't climb and you don't have a fill pump. The house stays as it is.

Store

Hardware store, both of you

One person holds the list and the photos. The other holds parts against the pump. Threads start by hand. Voltage matches. If the calculator says the water reaches, the fill pump stays on the shelf.

Yard first

Clear the front yard before you touch a pipe

The plants are in the way of the meter and of the path the new pipes will take. Gloves on. Cutter for the stems. Tongs for the scattered pieces you do not want to grab. Bags for the pile. Keep that pile off the meter.

Then the shovel. You are not trenching a ditch. You are moving enough soil to set the pipes in a shallow cover so they are not lying on top of the yard. Leave the meter valve and the pump easy to reach. Do not bury a joint you have not tested yet.

When the ground is clear, set the pump pad lower than the tank outlet, under cover. Look up before anyone walks with a long pipe.

Build

Assemble it while the house still has water

On the ground

Strainer, valves, flex lines, controller, shortcut, city one-way valve. Check the arrows. Support the pipes. Flush the tank bottom into a bucket before it reaches the pump.

On the tower

Float, overflow, coarse screen, lid. Strap the riser. The fill pipe ends above the overflow with air in between.

Before the cut

Eat, then walk the arrows

Hungry people rush a cut. Point at each arrow together. Say the words you'll use: closed, open, clear. The meter person does not leave the valve once it closes.

Water off

Tie it in

Follow the shutdown steps on Build. During the tie-in the shortcut stays closed and the pump stays unplugged. If this block starts late, your job is to restore water, not to finish the pump: unplug the pump, close the valve on the pump's way out, open the shortcut, open the meter, and prove one faucet.

Test

Leaks first, then power

On the ground

Open the meter slowly. Feel the joints. Prime until the water is solid. One faucet open, then plug in, then walk the house and the water heater.

On the tower

Watch the fill, the float, and the overflow. A running overflow means the float is stuck or the tank was already full.

Last half hour

Label it and stop

Do the shortcut drill once, then put it back. Unplug the pump. Close the valve on the pump's way out. Open the shortcut. Confirm a faucet still runs. Then close the shortcut, open the valve on the pump's way out, and plug the pump back in.

Tape labels. Photograph them. If you still have light, leave the shortcut closed, the pump plugged in, and one faucet proven. If the light is going, skip the polish: unplug the pump, close the valve on the pump's way out, open the shortcut, and go inside.

How it goes together

You are building two paths. One fills the tank. One goes from the tank, through the pump, into the house. A third pipe skips the pump, and its tap stays closed until the pump is unplugged.

Follow the water

City water climbs into the tank. The pump pushes it to the house. The backup pipe leaves at the tee and comes back at the joining tee. That tap stays closed while the pump is plugged in.

The four ball valves are the tank tap, the inlet valve, the valve on the way out, and the backup tap. The two tees are the split and the join. The two flex lines are the soft bends on either side of the pump.

A green Open is how that tap sits on a normal day. A red Closed is how the backup tap sits. Already there means the house or your sketch already has it. A dashed box is a branch, or a part you add only if the pump box has no one-way valve of its own.

Order of work

  1. Both · water still on

    Measure, photograph, test the valve

    Height, gauge, pipe sizes, outlet test. Bucket under the tank valve.

    Stop if the valve won't seal.

  2. Both · water still on

    Shop with the photos

    Match threads to the pump in the store. Read the cement can if you're buying cement.

  3. Ground · water still on

    Set the pump

    Paver, shelter, short fall from the tank, cord with a drip loop to an outlet that trips.

  4. Tower · water still on

    Float, overflow, screen, straps

    Fill pipe ends above the overflow. Lid on, loose enough to breathe.

  5. Ground · water still on

    Fit the valves, strainer, shortcut, and flex lines

    Tape on threads only. Support the pipes. If a joint is glued, it gets glued now so the wait happens while you do the rest. Flush the tank bottom into a bucket until it runs clearer, then connect the strainer.

  6. Both · water off

    Close the meter and tie in

    Faucet open until the flow dies. Bucket under the joint. Unscrew if it has threads. Connect the city side and the house side. Cap or close anything still open. The shortcut stays closed. The pump stays unplugged. Tape a note on the meter and tell the house to leave the taps alone.

    Stop if you can't see a way to close it back up. Cap the pipe, keep the meter closed, and think.

  7. Both · leak walk

    Open the meter slowly

    One faucet open. Walk every new joint with a dry hand. Watch the tank. If something weeps, close the meter, let it die, then fix it.

  8. Ground · power last

    Prime, then plug in

    Tank valve and inlet valve open. Prime plug open until solid water comes out, then closed. Open the valve on the pump's way out. One house faucet open, then plug in. Let it spit air, then run steady. Close the faucets and listen. It should stop and stay stopped.

    Unplug if it runs dry, keeps clicking, or the water heater starts dumping.

  9. Both · the drill

    Prove the shortcut, then put it back

    Unplug the pump. Close the valve on the pump's way out. Open the shortcut. Confirm a faucet still runs. Then close the shortcut, open the valve on the pump's way out, and plug the pump back in. Label the shortcut, the tank tap, and the valve on the pump's way out. Photograph the valves.

House tank › Safety

Stay safe

The meter stays open until the new parts are together and both of you are at the pipe.

Stay on your side of the meter

That valve is yours to close. The meter is the water company's. Cut or unscrew downstream of the valve, with a faucet open and a bucket under the joint.

Call every valve out loud

One person owns the meter during the shutdown and stays there. The other answers "clear" before a valve moves. The bad moment is a person holding an open pipe while the other opens the tank.

The pump gets water before it gets power

Open the tank side, crack the prime plug, wait until the water is solid, close the plug, then plug in. An empty pump cooks itself. If the pipe from the tank has a high loop, air will sit there and the pump will gulp. The pipe should fall the whole way.

Leave the air gap, the float, and the overflow

The gap keeps tank water out of the neighborhood line. The fill pipe ends in open air, so the tank has no way to push water back down it. The one-way valve by the meter is for the backup pipe, in case that tap is open while the pump is running. The float stops a quiet night from filling the tower forever. The overflow saves you when the float sticks. The tank breathes. A sealed drum is a different machine, and this plan is the wrong one for it.

Hands dry. Ground dry. Plug in last

The outlet has to trip when you press Test, and live again when you press Reset. Outside, water should not be able to run down the cord into the plug. The overflow should not be aimed at that outlet.

Look up before a long pipe goes up

A plastic pipe into an overhead line is a different kind of accident. If the tower takes both hands to climb, the person on the ground sends the tools up a rope.

Stay out of the tank

Work from the outside. Cement and primer stay in the open air, caps back on, off your skin. If a tower leg looks rotten, or the new pipe would hang off a cracked fitting, stop. The water can wait. A collapse can't.

Thread tape is only for threads

Wrap with the turn, three or four layers. Glue joints get primer and cement, and the time on the can. Push fittings get neither. Plastic threads crack if you lean on the wrench. Metal into plastic is hand-tight, then a nudge, with a second wrench holding the pump.

This tank is the water you'll use

Drinking-water pipe and fittings. Flush the sludge before it hits the pump. Let a faucet run before anyone drinks the first new water. Lid on. Coarse screen on the overflow.

Quit while you still have a way to get water

If the light is going and the pump isn't calm, put the house back on city water. Unplug the pump first. Close the valve on the pump's way out. Open the shortcut. Open the meter. Prove one faucet. Then stop.

A house with slow city water beats a half-finished pump in the dark.

House tank › Trouble

When something's wrong

If you need water and the pump is the problem, unplug it, close the tap on the pipe leaving the pump, then open the backup tap.

The tank tap only fills the tank. It does not feed the house. The pictures are on Plan.

Water is spraying
  1. Close the meter valve.
  2. Open a faucet so the pressure dies.
  3. Then fix the joint. Tightening a spraying fitting with your face over it is how people get cut and soaked.
  4. If you can't quiet it, cap the open pipe or close the nearest valve, and leave the meter closed until it's calm.
The pump won't start
  1. Press Reset on the outlet.
  2. Confirm the controller arrow points toward the house, and that no shutoff is closed between the tank and the pump.
  3. Open the prime plug until solid water comes out, close it, try again.
  4. If the outlet stays dead, use a different protected outlet. Leave the splices for an electrician.
It won't stop, or it keeps clicking
  1. Unplug it. A pump that runs all night burns. Clicking on and off does the same thing, slower.
  2. A toilet or a faucet is usually passing water. Fix that before you plug the pump back in.
  3. If every tap is truly closed and it still runs, leave it unplugged and open the shortcut so the house has city water. Close the valve on the pump's way out first.
The house has no water
  1. Check the closures: meter open, valve on the pump's way out open, shortcut closed, tank valve open.
  2. A one-way valve backward will block the whole house. The arrow points toward the house, or away from the meter on the city side.
  3. Air in the prime plug, or a high loop in the suction pipe, feels like "no water" too.
  4. For water this minute: unplug the pump, close the valve on the pump's way out, open the shortcut, open the meter, try a faucet.
The tank isn't filling
  1. Read the gauge again. If the city line can't lift water that high, the fill pump is the fix, and it can wait. The fill pump is not the tank tap.
  2. Put the house on the shortcut tonight. Unplug the pump, close the valve on the pump's way out, then open the shortcut.
  3. If the pressure is high enough and the level still doesn't rise, a valve is closed, the float is stuck up, or the old pipe is clogged. Push the float down. Open the tank tap (the fill valve on the climb).
The tank valve won't close
  1. Leave the house on the city line. Don't start the cutover.
  2. If it's already running, aim it somewhere that won't dig out the tower legs, and let the level drop until you can fit a new valve.
  3. Call the other person before anyone puts a hand in that stream.
It's getting late
  1. Unplug the pump.
  2. Close the valve on the pump's way out.
  3. Open the shortcut. That is the bypass valve, the shutoff that sends city water straight to the house.
  4. Open the meter.
  5. Confirm one faucet runs.
  6. Leave a label on the shortcut so nobody plugs the pump in while that shutoff is open. Go inside.

The pump's own paper wins if it disagrees with this page, especially the direction the controller has to stand. Feet of height ÷ 2.31 = psi to reach the inlet, and you want about 5 psi more. If the tower is rotten or the power means opening a wall, that part is a different day.
